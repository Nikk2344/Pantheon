## Why search needed help

Go offers many legal moves and long sequences of possible replies. Exhaustively exploring the game tree is impractical. A useful search must spend its effort selectively and estimate the value of positions before every possible continuation has been played out.

The 2016 AlphaGo paper brought neural policies and value estimates into Monte Carlo tree search. Its professional-player result was evidence about the combined system, not evidence that search or learning alone explained the achievement.

## Keep the versions distinct

The original system learned initially from human games and then improved through reinforcement learning. AlphaGo Zero, published in 2017, used self-play without human game examples. It still relied on engineered rules, architecture, search, and a training procedure. “Without human knowledge” should not be read as “without human design.”

## Work through an example

Imagine a position with twenty plausible moves. A policy points toward three promising candidates; look-ahead then reveals that the most immediately attractive one loses after a reply. An evaluator can help estimate the remaining positions. This simplified example explains the division of labour without reproducing AlphaGo's computations.

## What transfers beyond a board

The general research question is how to combine learned approximations with structured reasoning. In other settings, the hard part may be defining the reward or finding an accurate simulator. A game engine reliably tells you what follows a legal move. A real organisation does not come with such an oracle.

Compare [human-feedback training](/innovations/instructgpt), where judgments help define a learning signal, and [AlphaFold 2](/innovations/alphafold-2), where the target is a molecular structure rather than a winning move.
