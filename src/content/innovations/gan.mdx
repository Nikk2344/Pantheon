## A changing learning signal

A fixed distance between two pictures can penalise a plausible image just because its pixels differ from one particular target. The GAN proposal instead trains a discriminator using examples of real and generated data. Its feedback changes as the generator improves.

The models do not literally argue or understand deception. “Adversarial” describes their optimisation objectives. The discriminator estimates a source distinction, not whether an image tells the truth about the world.

## Why the elegant theory was not the whole story

The original paper analyses an idealised equilibrium in which generated and training distributions match. Finite networks trained by alternating updates are a different practical problem. Later work, including least-squares GANs, explored losses that could improve the learning signal and training behaviour.

## Work through an example

Suppose a dataset contains ten equally common handwritten digits. A generator produces beautiful sevens and nothing else. Someone shown three samples might admire the quality; someone counting the categories would notice nine missing classes. This is a useful mental model of mode collapse. Looking at a small, selected gallery cannot tell you whether the model covers the distribution.

## From a demonstration to a useful system

Ask how outputs were selected, how diversity was measured, and whether examples resemble training records too closely. These questions matter even if the pictures look realistic. The relevant standard depends on the application: design exploration, synthetic training data, and evidence about real events require very different judgments.

[Diffusion models](/innovations/diffusion) offer another generative approach, using repeated denoising instead of this original discriminator-generator contest.
