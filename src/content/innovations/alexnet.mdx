## The problem before the breakthrough

Recognition needs more than matching identical pixels. An object can move, rotate, or appear under different lighting. Earlier pipelines often relied on carefully designed features. AlexNet showed that a large supervised network could learn useful features while being trained for the classification task itself.

## What the experiment showed

The 2012 system combined convolution, rectified linear activations, data augmentation, dropout, and GPU computation. Its ImageNet competition submission achieved a top-five error of 15.3%, compared with 26.2% for the next entry. Top-five error asks whether the correct category appears among five predictions; it is not ordinary single-choice accuracy. See the original paper in the evidence list.

ImageNet was an enabling research contribution in its own right. Its organised categories and labelled images made training and comparison possible. The result should therefore be read as a story about data infrastructure and labour as well as algorithms.

## Work through an example

Imagine teaching a model to recognise mugs. A training set containing mostly mugs on wooden tables may reward a shortcut: detecting the table. Test the same mugs on grass, and the shortcut becomes visible. This thought experiment illustrates why changing the background is a useful evaluation, even when aggregate accuracy looks impressive.
