## Change the interface to a classifier

A conventional classifier chooses among labels fixed during training. CLIP learns a relationship between image and language representations. A user can describe candidate classes with text and compare their representations with that of an image.

The original paper trained on 400 million image-text pairs and tested transfer across a range of datasets. Such scale is part of the account: the system did not acquire visual concepts solely from the labels written at evaluation time.

## Work through an example

Imagine classifying pictures as “a photo of a bicycle” or “a photo of a motorcycle.” The image encoder supplies a vector; the text encoder supplies vectors for the two descriptions. Relative similarity helps determine a choice. This explanation omits implementation details but shows why changing the descriptions can change the task.

Now ask whether a pictured bicycle has exactly seven visible spokes. Success at broad categorisation would not answer that finer question. Each use needs an evaluation matched to its required precision.

## Similarity is not a complete account of a scene

The model card warns about deployment beyond tested uses and about uneven performance. Image-text associations can encode the assumptions and imbalances of their sources. A high similarity score is not a certification of identity, fairness, or factual accuracy.

CLIP itself is not a text-to-image generator. It computes representations and comparisons. Generative systems can use related representations as one component, but that does not make the encoder responsible for every part of the generated output.

## Compare two kinds of progress

[AlexNet](/innovations/alexnet) demonstrated learned visual features for a fixed benchmark. CLIP explored how language supervision can expand transfer. Both make the design of the evaluation as important as the appealing example.
