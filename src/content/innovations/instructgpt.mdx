## Prediction is not the same objective as assistance

A language model trained to predict text can continue a prompt in many ways. A user, however, may want a concise answer, a specific format, or an explicit acknowledgment of uncertainty. Additional training can make those intended behaviours more likely.

InstructGPT combined supervised demonstrations with a reward model learned from human rankings. It then used reinforcement learning to optimise responses against that reward. The published evaluation found that labelers could prefer a smaller instruction-tuned model over a much larger untuned baseline.

## Earlier preference-learning research

The 2017 human-preferences work investigated learning a reward function from comparisons of short behavioural segments. This is useful when writing the desired reward directly is difficult. InstructGPT applied a related idea to language responses; it did not invent all learning from feedback.

## Work through a comparison

Suppose two answers both contain the correct date. One gives it clearly; the other hides it in irrelevant prose. A labeler may prefer the first. But consider another pair in which an uncertain correct answer competes with a confident false one. Preference and correctness can diverge.

That distinction is why a useful evaluation should inspect multiple properties separately: instruction following, factual support, calibration, and performance across different users and contexts.

## The remaining question

A learned proxy is easier to optimise than the full purpose of a conversation. Ask whose judgments supplied the data, which prompts were represented, and how the system was tested outside those conditions. [Retrieval-augmented generation](/innovations/rag) addresses access to evidence, but retrieval and preference optimisation still do not make factual correctness automatic.
