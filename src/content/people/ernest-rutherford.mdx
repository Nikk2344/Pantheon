Rutherford grew up on a flax farm in New Zealand, one of twelve children, and left it
on a scholarship he won on the second attempt. He was, by every account including his
own, loud — a big, cheerful, booming man who sang "Onward, Christian Soldiers" tunelessly
around the laboratory, which his students used as an early-warning system for his mood.

## An experimentalist's instinct

What distinguishes Rutherford is not mathematical power; he had comparatively little and
cheerfully said so. It is his sense for the experiment that would force an answer. Faced
with a mysterious emanation, his response was to interpose foil and see what got through.
Faced with an atom whose interior was unknown, his response was to throw something hard
at it and watch the ricochet. The instruments were often crude — wrapped foil, a glass
tube, a screen that flashed when hit — but the questions were sharp enough that crude
instruments returned decisive answers.

## The thing that should not have happened

By 1909 the accepted picture was J. J. Thomson's: positive charge spread thinly through
the whole volume of the atom, with electrons embedded in it. Nothing that diffuse can
turn a heavy, fast alpha particle around. So when Rutherford asked Marsden to look for
alpha particles coming *backwards* off the foil, he was not testing a hypothesis. He was
handing a junior researcher a job that would almost certainly find nothing.

It found something. Rutherford spent the best part of two years working out what
distribution of charge could produce large deflections at exactly the observed rate, and
the answer was a tiny, dense, massive centre. The nuclear atom was not a guess that
happened to fit — it was reverse-engineered from a scattering distribution.

The simulation on this page reconstructs that reasoning step by step, with the scattering
genuinely computed rather than choreographed, so that the rarity of the reversal — the
actual evidence — is something you have to wait for.

## The chemist

Rutherford received the 1908 Nobel Prize in **Chemistry**, for his work on the
disintegration of the elements. He considered himself emphatically a physicist and
enjoyed the joke for the rest of his life, remarking that of all the transformations he
had studied, the quickest was his own from physicist to chemist.

He died in 1937 and is buried in Westminster Abbey, near
[Isaac Newton](/p/isaac-newton). Element 104 is named rutherfordium.
