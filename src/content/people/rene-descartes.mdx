discoveries:
  - title: The method of doubt, and the one thing that survived it
    year: 1641
    summary: >-
      Descartes' procedure was demolition before construction: withhold assent
      from anything not completely certain, exactly as carefully as from what is
      patently false, and do it wholesale rather than belief by belief. Three
      doubts do the work. The senses have deceived him, so nothing taken on their
      authority is safe. Dreams are not reliably distinguishable from waking, so
      neither is his body or the room he is sitting in. And an all-powerful cause
      could have made him defective enough to go wrong even in arithmetic — the
      one thing dreaming leaves untouched, and the reason the demon has to be
      brought on at all. What is left after that is that he is thinking, and that
      whatever thinks is not nothing.
    significance: >-
      This inverted the job description of philosophy. The question stopped being
      what the world is made of and became what a mind is entitled to claim,
      which is roughly what epistemology has been about ever since. The method
      also gave the sciences a template they still use — treat the received
      account as guilty until it survives a deliberate attempt to break it. But
      the reasoning has a shape that summaries flatten, and it is worth taking
      apart rather than being told about.
    argument: method-of-doubt
    disputed: >-
      "I think, therefore I am" does not appear in the Meditations. That book says
      "ego sum, ego existo" — I am, I exist. The French "je pense, donc je suis"
      is from the Discourse of 1637; the Latin "ego cogito, ergo sum" first turns
      up in the Principles of 1644; and the version usually quoted at students —
      "dubito, ergo cogito, ergo sum" — is not Descartes at all, but Antoine
      Léonard Thomas, writing in a prize essay in 1765.
  - title: Algebra and geometry made into one subject
    year: 1637
    summary: >-
      "La Géométrie", published as an appendix to the Discourse on the Method,
      took geometrical problems and rewrote them as equations. Descartes assigned
      letters to unknown lengths, and dropped the Greek rule that a squared
      quantity had to mean an area and a cubed one a volume — a power was just
      another length, so expressions could be built to any degree without
      becoming meaningless. He used this to attack Pappus' locus problem, and
      sorted curves into those an equation can capture and those it cannot. The
      notation is still on every blackboard: x, y and z for the unknowns, a, b
      and c for the constants, and exponents written as small raised numbers.
    significance: >-
      Once a curve is an equation, the whole apparatus of algebra can be turned
      on it — including finding tangents, which is the doorstep of calculus.
      Newton and Leibniz both worked from this. It is the reason a physical law
      can be written as a formula and drawn as a curve and understood to be the
      same object twice.
    disputed: >-
      The grid with two perpendicular axes and negative coordinates — the thing
      that actually gets called Cartesian — is nowhere in "La Géométrie".
      Descartes had the germ of it, in the two segments he labelled x and y while
      working the Pappus problem, and no more. The book was written in French
      rather than Latin, badly organised, and sketched rather than proved, partly
      by design: he left things out "in order to give others the pleasure of
      discovering them for themselves". Frans van Schooten and his students at
      Leiden did that discovering, in Latin editions from 1649 that ran to more
      than twice the length of the original.
  - title: The mind as a separate substance — and the question that broke it
    year: 1643
    summary: >-
      Having found that he could doubt his body but not his thinking, Descartes
      concluded that mind and body are distinct substances: one that thinks and
      takes up no space, one that takes up space and does not think. Everything
      physical, including the animal body, then runs as a machine. In 1643
      Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia wrote to ask the obvious question, and asked
      it precisely: how can something unextended move something extended, when
      every account of causal efficacy available tied that efficacy to extension?
      Either say what kind of causation this is, she told him, or say what the
      mind is such that the ordinary accounts cover it.
    significance: >-
      Elisabeth had located the load-bearing crack, and Descartes never repaired
      it. He fell back on the Scholastic notion of heaviness as a model for how
      an immaterial thing acts on a body — and she refused it, pointing out that
      he had himself argued such notions were unintelligible and incompatible
      with a mechanical view of nature. She pressed further: bad bodily states
      impair thought, which would be far easier to explain if the mind were
      extended after all. The mind–body problem in its modern form is that
      exchange, and their correspondence ran until his death.
    disputed: >-
      Descartes is routinely credited with, or blamed for, inventing the mind–body
      split, and the six-year correspondence in which it was taken apart is
      usually left out. Elisabeth was not a bystander to Cartesianism; she was its
      first serious internal critic, and "The Passions of the Soul" — written
      largely in response to her questions — is dedicated to her.
  - title: The law of refraction, and a world made of matter in motion
    year: 1637
    summary: >-
      "La Dioptrique" gave the rule relating the angles at which light enters and
      leaves a boundary, in terms of the sines, and put it to work on lens design.
      It belonged to a larger programme: Descartes wanted all of physics to be
      shape, size and motion, with no appeal to the qualities and tendencies of
      Scholastic natural philosophy. He had drafted a whole world on that plan in
      "Le Monde", then shelved it in 1633 on hearing what had happened to Galileo,
      and published the results piecemeal instead.
    significance: >-
      The refraction law is what makes lens design calculable rather than
      empirical, and it was the first substantial physical law to be recovered
      from the mechanical programme. That programme — nature as matter in motion,
      described mathematically — is the one Newton inherited, even as the Principia
      demolished the specific Cartesian physics of vortices.
    disputed: >-
      The law was found repeatedly before him: by Ibn Sahl in Baghdad in 984, who
      used it to work out the lens shapes that focus without aberration; by Thomas
      Harriot in 1602; and by Willebrord Snellius in 1621, whose name it carries
      and who never published it. Isaac Vossius accused Descartes in 1662 of
      having read Snell and manufactured a proof, and Fermat and Huygens took up
      the charge; the modern verdict is that it is undeserved and his derivation
      was his own. It was, however, a bad derivation — it assumed light travels
      infinitely fast and, inconsistently, faster in denser media. Fermat got the
      same law from the opposite assumptions, by requiring light to take the
      quickest path.

Descartes was educated by Jesuits at La Flèche, took a law degree to please his
father, and then did neither of the things those qualifications pointed at. He
enlisted instead — first in the Protestant Dutch army, later under a Catholic
duke on the other side of the same war — apparently because soldiering left him
free to think. What he was thinking about, after meeting the Dutch scientist
Isaac Beeckman in 1618, was mathematics.

## The stove

On the night of 10–11 November 1619, quartered at Neuburg an der Donau, he shut
himself in a room heated by a stove and had three dreams. He took them as a
revelation, and what he claimed to have seen was a single method: that the
certainty of mathematics was not a property of triangles but of a way of
proceeding, and that it could be applied to everything else.

That is a young man's conviction, and most young men's convictions do not
survive contact with the work. This one produced analytic geometry within
twenty years, which is a strong argument that he was onto something.

## Demolition

The method's most famous application is also its strangest. Descartes' problem
was that beliefs support each other — pull on one and you find it resting on
three more, and no amount of local repair tells you whether the whole structure
is sound. So he proposed to stop repairing and start demolishing: refuse assent
to anything that admits of the least doubt, as firmly as if it were false, and
see what is still standing at the end.

The demolition is done with three tools of escalating strength. The senses have
deceived him before, so nothing they report is safe. He might be dreaming, so
the room, the fire, the paper in his hand and the hand itself go too — and this
one is stronger than it looks, because it takes what is nearest rather than what
is furthest. But dreaming leaves arithmetic alone: two and three make five in a
dream. To reach that, Descartes needs something that could have built him wrong,
and this is where the malicious demon arrives — not as a horror story, but as a
device for doubting his own faculties. He is explicit that a *weaker* origin
would make the doubt worse, not better.

What survives is that he is thinking. The demon can deceive him about everything
except that there is a him to deceive.

## What the interactive is for

Everyone can quote the conclusion. Almost nobody can say what it rests on, and
the reason is that prose gives every premise the same apparent weight. Laid out
as a graph, the argument shows something a summary cannot: the dream and the
demon carry none of the load. Refuse both and the conclusion sits there
untouched. They make it *vivid*; two much duller lines make it *follow*.

What the doubt does carry is the next step — that the "I" is therefore a
thinking thing and nothing else. Hobbes went straight at that one in 1641: from
*I am thinking* you get *I am*, but "I am thinking, therefore I am thought" is no
better than "I am walking, therefore I am a walk", and the thing doing the
thinking might perfectly well be a body. Descartes' answer was partly a
concession — he had left the question open, he said, until the sixth Meditation.

objection, because knowing how a criticism was answered before you have felt its
force is not the same as understanding either.

## Sweden

<Aside>
  **The 5 a.m. lessons are supposed to have killed him.** Queen Christina had
  brought Descartes to Stockholm in 1649 to found an academy and teach her his
  views on love, and set tutorials for five in the morning, three days a week —
  a schedule that appears in almost every account of his death, because a man
  who reputedly worked in bed until noon being dragged out into a Swedish winter
  is a good story. It is a little too good. By 15 January 1650 the two had met
  only four or five times, and were not getting on. He fell ill on 1 February and
  died ten days later. Even the diagnosis is contested: pneumonia in most
  accounts, peripneumonia according to Chanut, in whose house he was staying —
  and Christina's physician van Wullen was refused permission to bleed him.
</Aside>

He was buried in a Stockholm graveyard used mostly for orphans, being a Catholic
in a Lutheran country. In 1663 the Pope put his works on the Index of Prohibited
Books; in 1671 Louis XIV banned the teaching of them in France. The remains were
returned to Paris in 1666 and moved again in 1819, arriving at
Saint-Germain-des-Prés without a finger and without the skull.

The vortices were wrong. The physics was superseded within two generations, by
[Isaac Newton](/p/isaac-newton), who had learned his mathematics from the Latin
editions of Descartes' geometry and then used it to take the Cartesian universe
apart. What outlasted the physics was the demand behind it — that a claim should
have to survive a deliberate, unsentimental attempt to break it before anyone is
obliged to believe it.
