Marie Skłodowska arrived in Paris in 1891 because Warsaw would not have her. Poland
was partitioned, its universities closed to women, and her education to that point had
come partly from the "Flying University" — an illegal night school that moved between
apartments to stay ahead of the Russian authorities. She enrolled at the Sorbonne,
lived on very little, and finished first in her physics degree.

## The problem she chose

In 1896 Henri Becquerel noticed that uranium salts fogged a photographic plate through
its wrapping. It was a curiosity, and largely treated as one — the field moved on to
Röntgen's more spectacular X-rays. Curie chose Becquerel's neglected effect as her
doctoral subject, and made one decision that determined everything after: she would
**measure** the radiation rather than photograph it.

The instrument mattered. Pierre and Jacques Curie had built an electrometer based on
piezoelectricity, capable of detecting the tiny currents produced when radiation
ionises air. It converted a qualitative glow into a reproducible number, and a number
is something you can compare across samples. Curie then did the unglamorous, decisive
thing: she tested every element and compound she could lay hands on.

## The inference

The result was a negative one, and it was the most important thing she ever found.
The activity of a sample tracked only the quantity of uranium or thorium in it. Heat
it, dissolve it, bind it into a different compound — the reading did not care. Chemical
processes work on the outer arrangement of atoms, so a radiation that ignores chemistry
cannot be coming from that arrangement. It had to come from the atom itself.

In 1898 that was close to heresy. "Atom" meant *uncuttable*; an atom with an interior,
capable of throwing pieces of itself away, was a contradiction in terms. Curie's
measurements made the contradiction unavoidable, and the atom's interior became the
central problem of the next forty years of physics — taken up, among others, by
[Ernest Rutherford](/p/ernest-rutherford), who would go looking for what was actually in there.

## What it cost

Nobody understood the danger. The Curies handled radium routinely, and were pleased by
its glow; Pierre deliberately burned his own arm with it to document the effect. Marie
carried samples in her pockets and kept them by her bed. She died in 1934 of aplastic
anaemia, a failure of the bone marrow consistent with prolonged radiation exposure.

Her laboratory notebooks are still radioactive. They are kept in lead-lined boxes at
the Bibliothèque nationale de France, and consulting them requires signing a waiver.
